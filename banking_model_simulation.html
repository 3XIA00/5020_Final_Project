The Banking Model
of Education

Paulo Freire warned us that education can become a system of deposits — filling students with approved knowledge, then measuring how well they can return it. Modern algorithms have industrialised this process.

In this simulation you will live one student's journey through standardised testing, college admissions, and graduate school — making choices, chasing scores, and only at the very end discovering what it cost you.

What This Simulation Shows — and What It Doesn't

"The more students work at storing the deposits entrusted to them, the less they develop the critical consciousness which would result from their intervention in the world as transformers of that world." — Paulo Freire, Pedagogy of the Oppressed

This simulation models how standardised testing algorithms — the SAT, GRE, and college admissions scoring systems — claim to measure academic merit objectively while systematically encoding the advantages of family income, school quality, and access to private tutoring. A student's genuine intellectual curiosity, randomly distributed across all income levels, has weak predictive power in determining final outcomes. Family income, compounding at every stage, has strong predictive power.

The simulation also tracks happiness — a variable the algorithm never considers. Time spent on personal passions, sports, and genuine deep learning contributes to flourishing. Time spent grinding test preparation, while it mechanically raises scores, diminishes wellbeing — particularly when it crowds out everything else. Notice that deep learning, while intellectually rewarding, carries a lower happiness weight than sports or personal interests: the banking model has transformed even genuine curiosity into a stressful, high-stakes performance, robbing it of much of its intrinsic joy.

What this simulation does not cover: This model intentionally brackets race and ethnicity, which research consistently shows carry additional score penalties not fully explained by income. It also does not model disability status, English-language learner status, geographic disadvantage beyond school quality, or the psychological toll of stereotype threat. A complete accounting of the banking model's unfairness would need to include all of these axes. We acknowledge them as real, significant, and beyond the scope of this project.

A note on data: All students in this simulation — including yours — are generated from random parameters, not drawn from real individuals or datasets. The formulas are calibrated to reflect documented patterns in educational research, but the specific outcomes you see are synthetic illustrations, not real-life cases. The simulation is a model, not a measurement.

The banking model's deepest harm is not only that it produces unequal outcomes — it is that it presents those outcomes as earned, laundering structural advantage through the language of merit and objectivity. The algorithm does not see the student. It sees a score.
